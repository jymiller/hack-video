Gatwick beats the thing I made up

You asked what the Meridian corpus is. It is invented — and your instinct is right: Gatwick is real, it is on video, and its covenants genuinely broke.

First, the honest answer

What the Meridian corpus is

I invented it. Yesterday.

Meridian Cold Storage Holdings does not exist. It was generated by one of Campaign Desk’s crews when I asked it to design a demonstration corpus for the DataHub entry — a fictional borrower with a $250m term loan, two fictional amendments, and a fictional CFO called Dana Whitfield signing a fictional compliance certificate.

The design is good: the certificate asserts compliance at 4.20× against a cited 4.50× limit, and it is wrong twice over, because an amendment both lowered the limit to 4.00× and capped an adjustment — making the true figure 4.48×. The obvious reading is confidently wrong, which is what a demonstration corpus is for.

Its weakness is fatal for video: a judge cannot verify a word of it, there is no footage of a company that does not exist, and it proves nothing about handling real-world mess. It lives in hack-datahub/docs/03-entry-design.md and nothing has been built from it.

Gatwick is the same story, except it actually happened — and the lenders actually had to waive the covenants.

Real bonds, real covenants, a real breach, and thousands of hours of real news footage sitting in the Internet Archive. Everything Meridian was pretending to be.

The credit structure is real

Gatwick is a leveraged borrower with public covenants

This is the part that makes it an Enid corpus rather than a general-interest one. Gatwick is financed exactly the way infrastructure credit is financed, and its covenants are disclosed.

£2.5bnListed secured bonds via Gatwick Funding Ltd, maturities 2024–2050
3.59×Senior interest cover ratio, year ended 31 Mar 2018 — down from 3.96×
£2.8bnReference net debt covered by the 2021 covenant waiver
WaivedSenior ICR and Senior RAR, not tested at Dec 2021 or Jun 2022

In August 2021 Gatwick went to its lenders and asked to be released from testing its interest cover and debt ratios. A very large majority agreed. That is a documented, public, real covenant event — the exact thing Enid exists to see coming, and it is the ending our demo can point at.

The trap case — and this one is real

Obvious reading: the airport was shut down by drones and it was on every news channel in the world, so that is the credit event.

Right reading: the drone cost Gatwick about £1.4m and moved nothing. The pandemic cost about £2bn and its lenders had to waive the covenants.

A system that reacts to the loudest footage gets this backwards. One that follows the footage through to passenger volumes, then to earnings, then to the ratio the bonds are tested on, gets it right. That is the whole argument for a context graph — and unlike Meridian, every number in it can be checked by a judge on their phone.

The archive

What is actually sitting there

I searched it rather than assuming. 894 items match Gatwick in the Internet Archive’s film collections, and the television news archive carries the December 2018 shutdown hour by hour as it happened.

Two corrections to what you said, and both help. Gatwick long predates 2000 — it has been a commercial airport since 1958, and an aerodrome since the 1930s, so historical newsreel of it exists. But the Internet Archive’s television news collection begins around 2009, which is the part that matters: it comfortably covers both the 2018 drone and the 2020 pandemic, which are the only two events that moved the credit.

So the long historical arc is available if we want it, but it is decoration. The credit story lives entirely inside the archive’s strongest window.

The demo

News footage in, covenant risk out

news footage— TwelveLabs → event · place · date · figures— written to → Neo4j graph— linked to → passenger volumes→ earnings→ Senior ICR

Speech gives us what the reporter says. OCR gives us the figures on the lower third and the studio graphics. Vision gives us the scene — a terminal full of stranded passengers reads differently from a press conference. All three write into one graph, and the graph is what connects a Thursday night in December to a ratio in a bond covenant.

The question no single clip can answer, and the graph answers instantly: “which of these events actually threatened the debt?”

The watchable moment

Drone footage playing, chaos in the terminal. A flat voice cuts in: “One thousand flights cancelled. One hundred and forty thousand passengers. Cost to the airport: one point four million. Interest cover unaffected. This is not the one.” Then the graph jumps to March 2020.

Carried on sound over footage the judge is already watching, and it says something counter-intuitive in the first fifteen seconds — which is what makes a stranger look up. It is not a graph drawing itself, which the desk refuses.

This fixes the DataHub entry too

If Gatwick works on Thursday, it should replace Meridian in the 10 August DataHub entry as well. One corpus, two hackathons, and the weakest part of the DataHub plan — a fictional borrower a judge cannot check — becomes its strongest.

The DataHub story survives the swap intact: an amendment or a waiver changes what a ratio means, lineage says which covenant tests depend on it. Gatwick’s actual 2021 waiver is a better instance of that than anything invented, because it really happened and the documents are public.
